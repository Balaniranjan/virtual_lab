EXPERIMENT 08 Hashing & Integrity

MD5 Message Digest Algorithm (RFC 1321): Compression & Collision Analysis

Study the 128-bit MD5 message digest function across its four 16-step rounds, evaluate avalanche sensitivity, and explore how collision vulnerabilities broke its integrity guarantees for cybersecurity.

Cryptographically Broken
Vulnerability Alert: MD5 has suffered catastrophic collision attacks since 2004 (Wang et al.) and was weaponized in 2012 by the Flame malware to forge digital certificates. MD5 is strictly prohibited for security-sensitive applications, digital certificates, and password storage. Modern Web Crypto intentionally does not support MD5.

Aim of the Experiment

To calculate, analyze, and test message digests using the RFC 1321 MD5 algorithm, evaluating its 64-step round operations and little-endian word architecture, while quantifying why collision vulnerabilities render MD5 insecure for modern cryptographic verification.

Key Learning Outcomes

  • Understand the 128-bit digest output and 512-bit block compression of RFC 1321.
  • Analyze the four auxiliary non-linear functions F, G, H, and I across 64 compression steps.
  • Recognize that native Web Crypto deliberately excludes MD5 due to security deprecation.
  • Quantify the Avalanche Effect and understand the impact of chosen-prefix collision attacks on digital signatures.

Theoretical Principles of MD5

1. Algorithmic Overview (RFC 1321)

Designed by Professor Ronald Rivest in 1991 to replace MD4, MD5 processes messages in 512-bit blocks (sixteen 32-bit words), using little-endian byte ordering, producing a 128-bit digest.

Four 32-bit State Registers:

A = 0x67452301, B = 0xEFCDAB89, C = 0x98BADCFE, D = 0x10325476

Four Non-linear Auxiliary Functions:

  • Round 1 (Steps 0-15): F(X, Y, Z) = (X ∧ Y) ∨ (¬X ∧ Z)
  • Round 2 (Steps 16-31): G(X, Y, Z) = (X ∧ Z) ∨ (Y ∧ ¬Z)
  • Round 3 (Steps 32-47): H(X, Y, Z) = X ⊕ Y ⊕ Z
  • Round 4 (Steps 48-63): I(X, Y, Z) = Y ⊕ (X ∨ ¬Z)

2. Collision Attacks & The Death of MD5

In August 2004, Chinese cryptanalysts Xiaoyun Wang, Dengguo Feng, Xuejia Lai, and Hongbo Yu published the first practical collision attack on MD5. They demonstrated that two distinct 128-byte messages could produce the identical MD5 digest in under an hour on a standard desktop.

In 2008, security researchers used chosen-prefix collisions to construct a rogue Certification Authority (CA) certificate trusted by all major web browsers. In 2012, the Flame cyber weapon used a colliding certificate to sign malicious binaries impersonating Microsoft Windows Update. Consequently, MD5 is completely deprecated for cryptographic integrity.

Simulation Procedure

  1. Navigate to the Practice tab.
  2. Enter any text message in the input text area.
  3. Click Calculate MD5 Digest.
  4. Inspect the resulting 32-character hexadecimal digest and confirm that its bit length is exactly 128 bits (16 bytes).
  5. Use the Avalanche Comparison Tool to compare hashes of two inputs differing by just one letter.
  6. Observe how the bit-level difference confirms the avalanche property, while understanding why collision resistance is broken.

EXPERIMENT 03 Symmetric Encryption

Data Encryption Standard (DES): Feistel Architecture & Round Analysis

Inspect the inner mechanisms of the 64-bit block cipher: initial permutation, 16 Feistel round transformations, S-box non-linear substitutions, subkey expansion, and inverse final permutation.

Legacy Standard (Withdrawn)
Obsolescence Notice: Single DES provides only a 56-bit effective key space (2^56 keys), which is trivially vulnerable to modern exhaustive brute-force search. It was withdrawn by NIST in 2005 and is taught solely to understand Feistel cipher structures.

Aim of the Experiment

To analyze and trace the internal architecture of the Data Encryption Standard (DES), deconstructing the 64-bit block Feistel rounds, 48-bit subkey derivation, 8 non-linear S-boxes, and evaluating the cryptanalytic reasons for its modern retirement.

Key Learning Outcomes

  • Understand the 64-bit block structure and 56-bit effective key size.
  • Trace Initial Permutation (IP), 16 Feistel rounds (L_i, R_i, K_i), and Final Permutation (FP).
  • Analyze the round function f(R, K): Expansion (E), XOR addition, 8 S-boxes, and Permutation (P).
  • Verify known test vectors from NIST Special Publication 800-20.

Theoretical Principles of DES

1. Architectural Overview

Adopted in 1977 as FIPS PUB 46, DES is a symmetric block cipher that processes data in 64-bit blocks under the control of a 64-bit key. The key contains 8 parity check bits (bits 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64), leaving 56 effective secret key bits.

2. The Feistel Cipher Structure

DES is built on Horst Feistel's network architecture. The 64-bit block is permuted by an Initial Permutation (IP) and then split into two 32-bit halves: L_0 and R_0.

Round Formulation (Rounds 1 to 16):

L_i = R_{i-1}

R_i = L_{i-1} ⊕ f(R_{i-1}, K_i)

Where ⊕ denotes bitwise XOR, and K_i is the 48-bit subkey derived for round i.

After Round 16, the halves are swapped in reverse order (R_16 || L_16) and passed through the Final Permutation (FP = IP^{-1}).

3. The Feistel Round Function f(R, K)

The round function f(R, K) executes four precise transformations on the 32-bit right half R:

  1. Expansion Permutation (E): Expands 32 bits into 48 bits by duplicating selected bits.
  2. Key Addition: Bitwise XOR of the 48 expanded bits with the 48-bit round subkey K_i.
  3. Substitution (S-Boxes): The 48 bits are partitioned into eight 6-bit chunks. Each 6-bit chunk enters one of eight non-linear S-boxes (S_1 to S_8). Bits 1 and 6 define the row (0 to 3), and bits 2 through 5 define the column (0 to 15), outputting 4 bits. This yields 8 × 4 = 32 bits.
  4. Permutation (P): Permutes the 32 bits from the S-boxes to achieve diffusion.

4. Why Feistel Ciphers are Invertible

A major mathematical elegance of the Feistel design is that the round function f(R, K) does not need to be invertible. Decryption runs the exact same Feistel rounds in identical sequence, merely applying the subkeys in reverse order (K_{16}, K_{15}, ..., K_1).

Simulation Procedure

  1. Navigate to the Practice tab.
  2. Select your input mode: ASCII Text or Hexadecimal (16 hex chars per block).
  3. Enter your input data in the Input Block field.
  4. Provide a 64-bit key (represented as 16 hexadecimal characters, e.g. 133457799BBCDFF1).
  5. Click Encrypt with DES.
  6. Examine the generated 64-bit ciphertext output and inspect the 16-Round Feistel Visualizer on the right.
  7. Inspect how each round generates subkey K_i, computes f(R, K), and updates L_i and R_i.
  8. Click Decrypt with DES to reverse the process and recover the original plaintext.

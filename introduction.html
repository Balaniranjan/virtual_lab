Introduction to Cryptography

Cryptography is the mathematical science of securing digital information against unauthorized interception, modification, and forgery in hostile communication environments.

Derived from the Greek words kryptos ("hidden") and graphein ("writing"), modern cryptography has expanded far beyond simple secret writing. It forms the core mathematical foundation for global e-commerce, cloud infrastructure, secure network protocols (TLS/HTTPS), blockchain systems, and identity management.

The Four Fundamental Security Goals

In academic information security, every cryptographic mechanism is designed to satisfy one or more of four fundamental security objectives:

1. Confidentiality

Ensures that information is inaccessible to unauthorized entities. Achieved through symmetric (AES) and asymmetric (RSA) encryption algorithms.

2. Integrity

Guarantees that data cannot be modified, corrupted, or tampered with in transit without detection. Achieved through cryptographic hash functions (SHA-256) and MACs.

3. Authentication

Verifies the claimed identity of the transmitting party or origin of data. Achieved through digital signatures (DSS, ECDSA) and public-key certificates.

4. Non-Repudiation

Prevents the creator of a message from falsely denying having sent it, providing irrefutable mathematical evidence to third parties.

Core Terminology & Transformation Pipeline

Term Notation Academic Definition
Plaintext P or M The original, unencrypted intelligible message or data payload.
Ciphertext C The transformed, unreadable encrypted data produced by applying an encryption algorithm.
Key K A piece of information (parameter) that controls the operation of the cryptographic algorithm.
Encryption E_K(P) = C The mathematical process that converts plaintext into ciphertext using a key.
Decryption D_K(C) = P The inverse mathematical process that recovers the plaintext from ciphertext using a key.

Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Cryptography

Symmetric Cryptography (Secret-Key)

Both communication parties share a single, identical secret key for both encryption and decryption: D_K(E_K(P)) = P.

  • Advantages: Extremely high computational throughput; ideal for bulk data encryption.
  • Challenge: Secure key distribution (how to transmit the shared key securely without prior trust).
  • Examples: DES (historical), AES (modern standard), ChaCha20.

Asymmetric Cryptography (Public-Key)

Each entity possesses a mathematically linked keypair: a Public Key freely distributed for encryption, and a Private Key kept strictly secret for decryption.

  • Advantages: Solves key distribution; enables digital signatures and non-repudiation.
  • Challenge: Computationally intensive; typically 1,000x slower than symmetric ciphers.
  • Examples: RSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECDSA, Ed25519.

Historical Evolution of Cryptographic Design

Era / Class Underlying Primitive Key Algorithms Security Level
Classical Ciphers Alphabet substitution & transposition Caesar, Playfair, Hill, Rail Fence Trivial to break via frequency analysis & linear algebra
Early Block Ciphers Feistel network, 56-bit keys Data Encryption Standard (DES) Obsolete (vulnerable to brute-force key search)
Modern Symmetric Substitution-Permutation Network, Galois Fields AES-128, AES-256 (GCM mode) High Security (resistant to linear/differential cryptanalysis)
Public-Key & Signatures Number theory (Factoring, Discrete Log, Elliptic Curves) RSA-2048, Diffie-Hellman, DSS / ECDSA High Security (when parameters exceed 2048 bits / 256-bit ECC)
